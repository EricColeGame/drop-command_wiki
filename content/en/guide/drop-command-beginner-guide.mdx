## Piloting Any Unit: Why That Matters

Most mech games ask you to pick a chassis before the match and live with it. Drop Command's official description instead says you can pilot any unit under your command. Read that without adding numbers: it describes flexibility of control, not a guarantee that every unit is equally strong or equally available at all times.

The practical consequence is that your attention becomes the real resource. In small skirmishes you may only have a handful of units, so piloting one directly is straightforward. As battles scale toward the "epic" end the page mentions, the question shifts from "what do I pilot" to "what do I pilot right now, and what do I let the rest of my force handle."

This is general reasoning from the combined-arms premise, not measured data. No one outside the developer can tell you which unit wins a specific matchup, because the store page publishes no such tables.

## Combined Arms and the Evolving Battlefield

Combined arms is a real military concept: different arms cover each other's weaknesses. The Steam description names Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour as four personalisable categories. That is the clearest structural hint the page gives.

The evolving battlefield detail is equally important. Fully destructible debris and unit wreckage mean the map you started on is not the map you finish on. Rubble can block routes; wreckage can mark where fighting already happened. Because the page does not document whether debris blocks line of sight, slows movement, or can be cleared, the honest answer is that you should watch for it during your first matches and form your own view.

Tactically, the safe assumption rooted in the premise — not in any published figure — is that a force with only one arm is more predictable than a mixed one, and that a battlefield you have altered is one you should re-read rather than assume.

## Veterans, Rookies, and Pilot Progression

The store page offers two named approaches: building squads of veterans, or rushing opponents with rookies. It also confirms pilots are customisable and levelable.

What it does not say is how much experience a pilot needs per level, what a level changes, or whether a leveled pilot can be lost permanently. Those are undocumented. You can verify them in-game by checking your pilot screen after a few missions, or by looking for a manual or patch notes on the official page.

The design tension is familiar. Veterans likely represent invested time that you would rather not throw away; rookies are, by the page's own wording, disposable. Treat that as an interpretation of the description, not a rule. Where the game draws the line between the two is something only play will show you.
