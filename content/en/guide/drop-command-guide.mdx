Drop Command is a large-scale mech combat game from developer PillBugGames, released on Steam on 28 September 2026. Its pitch is unusually broad: you do not lock into a single chassis or a single role. You pilot any unit under your command, and the scale of the fight can swing from a small skirmish to a full battle line. That flexibility is the whole appeal, and it is also the thing most likely to confuse a new player. This article sticks to what the official Steam page states, separates that from community impressions, and offers general reasoning about how to approach the game rather than invented numbers.

You can find the official store listing at https://store.steampowered.com/app/1944880/ — that page is the authority for the game's advertised features, and it is worth reading before you buy or before you trust any third-party summary.

## What the Steam page actually advertises

The verified description covers several distinct promises. First, combined-arms combat at a grand scale. Second, the ability to pilot any unit under your command. Third, dropping into combat alone, leading a squad, or commanding an army. Fourth, an evolving battlefield with fully destructible debris and unit wreckage. Fifth, customisable and levelable pilots. Sixth, the choice between elite squads of veterans and disposable rookies. Seventh, personalisation of Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour.

That is the complete list of advertised pillars. Notice what is missing: the store page does not publish unit stats, damage tables, build costs, tech trees, faction lists, or any kind of code system. If a guide online presents those as fact, treat it as a third-party observation at best, and possibly as guesswork. The developer has not documented them on the product page.

The four unit categories named — Mech, Tank, VTOL, Battle Armour — are the only ones confirmed. You should not assume there are others, and you should not assume the naming implies specific stat profiles. A VTOL is a VTOL; the page does not say how fast or how fragile.

## Piloting any unit changes how you should think about role

The headline feature is that you can pilot any unit under your command. In most mech games you are the mech, and everything else is scenery or support. Here, the player is a commander who can also take the controls. That single design fact reshapes tactics.

If you can jump into any unit, then your value as a player is not tied to one chassis. You can move to where the fight is failing. That suggests a few general habits, framed as reasoning from the premise rather than measured advice:

- Keep a mental map of which unit type is under pressure at any moment, because you may be the best reinforcement available.
- Treat your own piloting time as a scarce resource and spend it where a human decision beats an AI one.
- Avoid committing personally to a fight that a subordinate unit could win without you.

None of this is documented as a mechanic. It is simply what follows from "pilot any unit under your command" as the Steam page describes it. Verify the real behaviour in-game.

## Veterans, rookies, and the squad economy

The page explicitly offers "elite squads of veterans or disposable rookies." That is a design choice about attrition. Veterans presumably survive longer and perform better; rookies are cheap and expendable in the sense the page implies.

Two readings are possible, and the page does not settle which is true. One is a persistent roster where you invest in named pilots over time and lose them permanently. The other is a per-mission pool where veterans and rookies differ only in starting quality. The phrase "customisable and levelable pilots" points toward persistence, but the page does not confirm perma-death, respec rules, or experience curves.

## The evolving battlefield and wreckage

"Fully destructible debris and unit wreckage" is one of the more concrete claims. Destroyed units leave wreckage, and the battlefield changes as the fight goes on. In combined-arms games this usually means cover shifts, lanes close, and what was open ground becomes cluttered.

General reasoning: do not plan a route once and commit to it. Reassess as debris accumulates. Wreckage that blocks a tank may not block Battle Armour. Conversely, wreckage that gives you cover may give the enemy cover on the next push. The page does not describe how wreckage is cleared, whether it blocks line of sight, or whether it can be removed. Confirm in-game.

## Personalisation without published stats

The page names four chassis types and says they are personalisable. It does not say what "personalisation" includes. It could be cosmetic, it could be modular loadouts, it could affect performance. Do not assume a build system with published numbers, because none are published.

If customisation is mechanical, the sensible approach is to specialise rather than generalise. Combined-arms games reward units that do one job well over units that do several jobs poorly. But that is a general principle, not a Drop Command fact. Check the in-game customisation screen, and check the official page for updates, since post-launch patches may add detail the page does not yet carry.
