Drop Command is a mech combined-arms combat game from developer PillBugGames, released on Steam on 28 September 2026. Its hook is scale plus direct control: you pilot any unit under your command, and the fight can be a small skirmish or a full battle. You can drop into combat alone, lead a squad, or command an army, on a battlefield that evolves as debris and wreckage accumulate.

That is the shape of the game as described on its [official Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1944880/). This guide sticks to what the developer advertises, separates that from community impressions, and is honest about what is not documented. Where it offers tactical advice, that advice is reasoning from the combined-arms premise, not measured data.

## What PillBugGames Actually Advertises

According to the game's official description, Drop Command is built around several pillars:

- **Grand-scale mech combat with combined arms.** You are not locked to one chassis type. Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour are all listed as personalisable units.
- **Pilot any unit under your command.** You can take direct control of a unit rather than only issuing orders from above.
- **Scales from skirmish to epic battle.** The same game supports dropping in alone, leading a squad, or commanding an army.
- **An evolving battlefield.** Fully destructible debris and unit wreckage change the environment as the fight continues.
- **Customisable, levelable pilots.** Pilots can be developed over time.
- **Elite squads or disposable rookies.** You can build veteran squads or rush opponents with rookies.
- **Unit personalisation.** Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour can be modified.

What the description does not include matters just as much. The store page does not publish unit stats, damage tables, build costs, tech trees, faction lists, or a code system. Anyone presenting exact numbers as official is going beyond the source.

## Combined Arms as a Decision Framework

Combined arms is not a buzzword here; it is the core decision loop. The advertised roster spans ground, air, and infantry-scale options, and each has natural strengths and weaknesses. General reasoning suggests the following, and you should treat it as a starting hypothesis rather than a rulebook:

| Unit type | Likely role in a combined-arms force | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Mech | Flexible frontline anchor | Piloted units in mech games usually trade speed for durability and firepower |
| Tank | Ground pressure and direct fire | Tracked vehicles typically favor stability and sustained ground combat |
| VTOL | Mobility, flanking, air presence | Aircraft commonly exploit vertical space and reposition quickly |
| Battle Armour | Infantry-scale support | Smaller units often fill gaps, screen, or operate in tight spaces |

The table is inference from the genre and the advertised unit list, not from published statistics. The practical takeaway is to avoid mono-type forces. If you commit only Mechs, you may lack the speed or reach another unit type provides. If you commit only VTOLs, you may struggle to hold ground.

## Dropping Alone, Leading a Squad, or Commanding an Army

The description explicitly supports all three command scales. Each changes what you should pay attention to:

- **Alone.** You are the whole force. Positioning, timing, and knowing when to disengage matter more than raw output.
- **Squad.** You have a handful of units to coordinate. The value of a squad is covering each other's weaknesses, so composition and spacing become the priority.
- **Army.** At this scale, you cannot personally control every engagement. You will rely on unit grouping and broad orders, and you should expect to pick the fights that decide the battle rather than win every one.

Because the game lets you pilot any unit under your command, a useful habit is to switch into the unit that currently matters most: the one holding a key position, the one that can break a stalemate, or the one about to be lost. That is general combined-arms reasoning, not a documented mechanic.

## Veterans or Rookies: A Trade-Off, Not a Rule

PillBugGames advertises two paths: building squads of veterans, or rushing opponents with rookies. These imply different curves.

Veteran squads presumably take time and investment to assemble but should perform more reliably in sustained fights. Rookie rushes presumably trade long-term quality for immediate numbers or tempo. Neither is stated to be stronger. The honest position is that the balance depends on tuning the developer has not published, so you should test both in-game rather than trust a tier list.

If you want to know how pilot leveling affects performance, the only reliable method is to level a pilot yourself and compare behavior in the same situation. The store page does not quantify it.

## The Evolving Battlefield

Destructible debris and unit wreckage are advertised as part of the battlefield evolution. In practice, this likely means the terrain you fight over changes as the battle proceeds: cover can be created or removed, and wreckage can shape movement lanes.

Practical implications, framed as reasoning rather than confirmed rules:

- Do not assume a firing position stays viable for the whole match.
- Treat fresh wreckage as an unknown: it may block, channel, or shelter movement.
- Re-check sightlines after major engagements rather than relying on an early read.

Because the page does not document collision behavior or cover values, verify these in a controlled skirmish before relying on them in a large battle.
