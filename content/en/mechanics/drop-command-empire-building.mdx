## Why "Combined Arms" Changes How You Should Think

Combined arms is a military concept: different unit types cover each other's weaknesses rather than operating alone. The store page names four categories, Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour, which suggests the four fill different roles in that relationship.

The exact roles are not documented, so the following is general reasoning from the premise, not measured data:

- Tanks typically trade mobility for durability and direct fire, making them natural anchors or line-holders.
- VTOLs suggest air mobility, which usually means reaching positions ground units cannot, or pressuring targets from unexpected angles.
- Battle Armour implies smaller infantry-scale units, often useful for holding ground, scouting, or operating where vehicles are awkward.
- Mechs sit at the centre of the game's identity and likely serve as flexible heavy hitters.

Since you can pilot any unit under your command, the tactical question is not "which unit is strongest" but "which unit should I be in right now." If your line is collapsing, piloting the unit holding it may matter more than piloting the unit doing the most damage. That reasoning is inferred from the combined-arms framing and is worth testing yourself once you are in the game.

## Piloting Any Unit: A Practical Way to Decide

The ability to jump into any unit you control is unusual and deserves its own approach. A simple method:

1. Decide the battle's current problem, such as a stalled push, a threatened flank, or an uncontested objective.
2. Identify which unit in your force addresses that problem.
3. Take direct control of that unit rather than micromanaging from above.
4. Hand back control once the situation stabilizes and return to the broader view.

This works because your attention is the scarcest resource in a large battle. The store page does not document a specific commander camera, order system, or hotkey layout, so confirm how control swapping actually works in your own session. Practising it early is cheaper than learning it mid-battle.

## Veterans, Rookies, and Managing Attrition

The description explicitly offers "elite squads" or "disposable rookies." That phrasing tells you attrition is a designed part of the game, not a failure state. It also implies pilots can be levelled, so a veteran is an investment.

A reasonable default: use rookies for tasks where losses are likely and acceptable, and keep veterans for the fights you cannot afford to lose. Because build costs, XP curves, and loss penalties are not published anywhere official, you cannot plan exact budgets in advance. What you can do is watch how your own veteran squads perform over several battles and adjust from observation. General reasoning about attrition management is useful, but it is not a guarantee of results.

| Situation | Reasonable default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Probing an unfamiliar map | Rookies or cheap units | Information is worth more than the units |
| Holding a critical position | Veterans | Experience compounds when units survive |
| Testing a new loadout | Rookies | Limits the cost of a bad experiment |
| Late in a campaign you care about | Veterans | Preserves accumulated investment |

## Customization and the Evolving Battlefield

Pilots are customisable and levelable, and Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour are all personalisable. The store page does not publish a tech tree, a parts list, or a code system, so ignore any site claiming to list them. The right way to learn the customization system is to build something modest, take it into a low-stakes fight, and note what actually changed.

A lane that was open at the start of a fight may be cluttered later, and wreckage may reshape how ground units move. Watching the battlefield change, rather than memorizing a map, is likely to matter more than any single opening build.
