Drop Command is a large-scale mech combat game from developer PillBugGames, released on Steam on 28 September 2026. The pitch is unusually broad for the genre: you do not pick one iconic war machine and live inside it for twenty hours. Instead, you pilot any unit under your command, and that command can be as small as yourself or as large as an army. That single design decision shapes almost everything else about how the game plays, and it is the detail most worth understanding before you spend your first hour in it.

This article sticks to what PillBugGames actually advertises on the official Steam page, separates that from what players report, and is honest about what the store never documents.

## The advertised design in plain terms

The official [Drop Command store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1944880/) describes combined arms combat at a grand scale, where you drop into battle alone, lead a squad, or command an army. The battlefield is described as evolving, with destructible debris and unit wreckage. Pilots can be customised and levelled. You can build squads of veterans or rush opponents with rookies. Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs and Battle Armour are all listed as personalisable unit types.

Three things follow from that wording, and they matter more than any individual stat:

- **Unit switching is core, not a bonus feature.** If you can pilot any unit under your command, your moment-to-moment decisions include which seat to occupy, not just where to move.
- **Scale is a variable you set.** The same game supports a lone drop and a full army command, which implies the challenge comes from force composition and positioning rather than a fixed difficulty curve.
- **The map is a resource and a hazard.** Wreckage and destructible debris are advertised as part of an evolving battlefield, so terrain changes as the fight continues.

## Reading combined arms as a planning problem

Because PillBugGames has not published unit stats, damage tables, or build costs, any advice here is reasoning from the premise rather than measured data. Treat it as a framework for your own testing.

Combined arms works when each unit covers another's weakness. Tanks generally want open ground and long sightlines. VTOLs want vertical space and the ability to disengage. Battle Armour suggests close, small-scale work where a full-size vehicle is awkward. Mechs sit somewhere between armour and infantry in most settings, and in this game they are the most directly personalisable option.

The practical question is not "which unit is best" but "which unit should I be in right now." A useful habit is to ask, before each engagement:

1. Which units in my force are exposed if I stay in my current seat?
2. Which enemy unit is causing the most trouble, and which of my units counters it?
3. If I switch, what breaks in the next thirty seconds?

That third question is the one players forget. Leaving a command position to personally handle a threat can be correct, but the official description implies you are responsible for everything under your command, so a heroic solo run that costs you the wider fight is a bad trade.

| Situation | Reasonable default | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Small skirmish, few units | Pilot the unit best suited to the terrain | Little coordination overhead; personal skill matters most |
| Mixed force, open ground | Stay in a command-capable unit | Rewards positioning and keeps you able to react |
| Enemy air pressure | Consider VTOL or anti-air-capable units | Vertical mobility changes the engagement |
| Cluttered, broken terrain | Battle Armour or a smaller unit | Debris and wreckage constrain large vehicles |

Those pairings are inference from the advertised unit types, not documented rules.

## Pilots, veterans and rookies

The store page states that pilots are customisable and can be levelled, and that you can build squads of veterans or rush opponents with rookies. That framing suggests a genuine trade-off rather than a straight upgrade path. Veterans represent accumulated investment; rookies represent cheap, replaceable volume.

Since no experience rates, level caps, or progression tables are published, you cannot plan around specific numbers from outside the game. What you can do is decide your risk posture. If you intend to absorb losses, rookie-heavy forces are consistent with that. If you intend to preserve a core of experienced pilots, you will want to avoid putting them in the most exposed roles. The game's own description presents both as legitimate, which is a reasonable signal that neither is a trap.

## Customisation and verification

Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs and Battle Armour are all described as personalisable. The Steam page does not say how deep that customisation goes, whether it is cosmetic, functional, or both, and it publishes no build costs or module lists. Anyone claiming to know exact part values is working from something other than the official listing.

To check for yourself, open the [official Steam page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1944880/) and read the full feature list, then compare it against what the game shows you in a customisation screen. If a system is not described there and not visible in the interface, it is undocumented rather than confirmed.

## Community observations and their limits

Third-party guides and forum posts have begun appearing since release, and some are useful for orientation. The caveat is that they are secondary observations, not developer documentation. A guide writer may have tested one build path on one map and generalised from it. That does not make the advice wrong, but it does mean you should treat specific numbers, tier lists, or "best unit" claims as one player's experience rather than a specification.

Where third-party material is strongest is in describing feel: how a unit handles, how quickly a battle escalates, how much micromanagement a large force demands. Where it is weakest is in anything numeric.
