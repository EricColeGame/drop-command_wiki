## The four unit classes and what "any unit" implies

The phrase "pilot any unit under your command" is the design spine. The store description names four chassis families: Mechs, Tanks, VTOLs, and Battle Armour. It does not say how many variants exist per family, how they are unlocked, or whether every unit is pilotable from day one.

What we can reason about is role separation. In combined-arms games, the standard trade-off usually runs along three axes: speed and mobility, durability, and range or directness of firepower. VTOLs tend to control the third dimension and react fastest. Tanks usually trade mobility for staying power. Battle Armour sits at the small, cheap, hard-to-hit end. Mechs sit in the middle-to-heavy band and tend to be the most modular.

## Why destructible debris changes how you should think

The store page calls the battlefield "evolving," with "fully destructible debris and unit wreckage." That is a claim about the environment, but it has tactical consequences worth reasoning through.

If debris persists, then cover is not fixed at match start. A wall you used on turn one may be rubble by turn four, and a fresh pile of wreckage may create new cover where none existed. Two habits follow from that premise:

1. Do not build your plan around one piece of hard cover for the whole fight.
2. Watch where wrecks accumulate. In persistent-debris games, wreckage often becomes a chokepoint that channels movement.

Fully destructible environments also tend to reward area weapons and punish static defense. None of that is confirmed mechanics; it is what the advertised destructibility implies for a player who takes the description at face value.

## Veterans versus rookies: the squad-design question

The store text offers two squad philosophies: "elite squads or disposable rookies." It does not publish a veterancy curve, experience values, or casualty penalties.

Reading it as a design statement, the game appears to want both approaches to be legitimate. That usually means the trade-off is about loss tolerance rather than raw power. A veteran squad concentrates capability into a few units you cannot easily replace. A rookie swarm spreads risk but depends on numbers and coordination.

A practical framing, not a guarantee of success:

- Early on, when you have little to lose, low-cost units let you learn the map and the unit families without much cost.
- As your named pilots accumulate levels, protecting them matters more than spending them.
- Because pilots are "customizable and levelable," personnel are likely a separate resource from hardware in some way. Whether that means permanent death, injuries, or something softer is not stated on the store page.

## Building a battle plan from the premise

Combining the advertised pieces gives a reasonable mental model, again as general reasoning rather than tested strategy:

- Drop in alone when you want to learn a unit family's handling without risking a squad.
- Lead a squad when you need combined arms but want direct control of the decisive moment.
- Command an army when the objective is broad and your attention is better spent on positioning than on aiming.

Because you can pilot any unit under your command, the real skill question is switching cost: how quickly you can hand off one unit and take direct control of another when the battle turns. Practicing that handoff is probably more valuable than mastering one chassis, but that is an inference from the design promise, not a documented mechanic.
