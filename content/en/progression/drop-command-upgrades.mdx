## The Combined-Arms Premise in Practice

Combined arms is a real military concept: different unit types cover each other's weaknesses. Since Drop Command explicitly names four unit categories and lets you command any of them, the natural reading is that no single chassis dominates every situation.

This is general reasoning from the premise, not measured data. But it gives you a sensible way to approach fights:

- **Mechs** are the genre's traditional all-rounders, typically trading speed for durability and firepower.
- **Tanks** usually favour ground armour and direct fire.
- **VTOLs** imply air mobility, which tends to mean speed and vertical angles at the cost of staying power.
- **Battle Armour** suggests small, infantry-scale units — cheap, numerous, or specialist.

None of those traits are confirmed for Drop Command specifically. The point is that if the game means what "combined arms" says, mixing categories should cover gaps that a single-type force leaves open. The best way to confirm this is to run the same scenario twice with different compositions and watch what breaks.

## Reading the Evolving Battlefield

"Evolving battlefield with fully destructible debris and unit wreckage" is one of the more concrete claims in the description, and it has tactical consequences even without numbers. If debris and wreckage persist, cover is not static. A position that was clean at the start of an engagement may be cluttered later — or a destroyed unit may itself become an obstacle.

The honest caveat: PillBugGames has not published whether wreckage blocks movement, blocks line of sight, can be cleared, or affects nothing but visuals. Those are exactly the questions to test in a low-stakes skirmish before committing an army. Load a small fight, destroy something large, and observe whether your units path around it.
